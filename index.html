About Kendall

From the track
to the soil

I was an elite runner before chronic pain took that away for twelve years. In the process of losing my athletic identity I found something more durable — a faith stripped of its performance layer, a love for growing things slowly, and a deep conviction that meaning is available almost anywhere if you're willing to pay attention.

I live in Utah with my wife and daughter. I grow perennials, vegetables, fruit, and flowers. I write about what the garden teaches me about everything else.

About Kendall Thompson

I used to be a runner.
Now I grow things.

The story of how twelve years of chronic pain, an identity crisis, and a patch of Utah soil made me the man I am.

My name is Kendall Thompson, and for most of my life I've been searching for meaning.

I grew up in Utah surrounded by mountains, faith, and the ordinary rhythms of life that seem insignificant until years later when you realize they shaped who you became. I was a runner — a serious one. Track and cross country. The kind of athlete who felt most like himself at mile six of a ten-mile tempo run, when everything hurt just enough and the rhythm took over and the world got very quiet and very clear.

"My body was the thing I trusted most. I knew what it could do. I knew its limits and I knew how to push past them. My identity lived there — in the pace, in the times, in the feeling of crossing a finish line having left everything on the course."

Then chronic pain arrived. Quietly at first — the way a houseguest shows up before you realize they're not leaving. I kept running because running was what I was. I ran through the early discomfort the way I'd run through every other hard thing — head down, jaw set, certain my body would cooperate if I just demanded it to.

My body had other plans.

The Decade

Ten years is a long time to be in pain. I want to say that plainly because I think we soften these things and the softening does a disservice to anyone going through something similar.

It was hard. Progressively, relentlessly hard. The kind of hard that changes your personality, your relationships, your faith, your sense of who you are and what your life is for. I watched the running go first. Then the easy movement. Then the assumption that tomorrow would feel better than today.

What I didn't expect was how thoroughly chronic pain would get into everything. Producing anxiety. Producing grief. A particular kind of loneliness that comes from suffering that is invisible and hard to explain. And the loss of my athletic identity left me with a question I wasn't equipped to answer: Who are you when the thing that defined you is gone?

Those questions became more than philosophy. They became survival. I searched everywhere — in faith, science, theology, philosophy, psychology, in books read at 2am, in prayers that felt like shouting into a room I wasn't sure had anyone in it.

Finding the Soil

I found the garden when I couldn't find anything else. Not through a dramatic decision — I just needed something my body could manage. Something that required presence without requiring performance. Something that gave back without demanding more than I had.

The soil asked nothing of me that I couldn't give. It didn't care how fast I used to run. It just needed attention, consistency, a willingness to show up even when nothing visible was happening yet.

"The garden became the first place in years where I felt like myself. Not my old self. Something newer. Something that had grown in the hard years without my realizing it."

I grow perennials mostly — things that come back. Vegetables because there's something quietly sacred about eating what you grew. Fruit because patience has a flavor. Flowers because beauty is not a luxury.

The Miracle

After roughly twelve years the pain began lifting. Slowly. Without a clear medical explanation. After years of searching, treating, praying, and enduring — one day it started getting better. I don't fully know why. I can only call it grace.

I prayed about this for twelve years — not always faithfully or eloquently, sometimes the way you talk to someone in a room when you're not sure they're listening. I kept talking. And then something changed. Maybe that's what a miracle looks like. Not lightning. Just the ground thawing when you had started to accept the cold.

    Rooted in Utah

    Welcome to
    The Garden

    A place for growth, becoming, and staying rooted in what matters.

    The Garden is the community heart of Rooted in Utah. It is not a content feed or a list of values. It is an invitation — into a philosophy of growth that moves through every season of life, and a community of people committed to tending what matters most.

    Scroll to explore
    Stage One

    The Seed

    Every meaningful life begins impossibly small. A single decision. A quiet hope. A moment of faith before anything is visible.

    Faith Hope Curiosity Beginning Courage

    A seed carries everything it will ever become inside something too small to see. The oak is already in the acorn. The garden is already in the packet. The life is already in the decision — even when the decision feels fragile and uncertain and not quite like enough.

    The beginning is always the hardest part — not because the work is heaviest there, but because the evidence hasn't arrived yet. You are asked to act before you can see. That is what faith actually is.

    In The Garden, we honor beginnings. We make space for people who are just starting — a new season of faith, a first step toward health, a decision to tend something they've been neglecting. Every person here was once a seed.

    Stage Two

    The Roots

    The most important growth is the kind nobody sees. The difficult middle. The underground season. Strength built in the dark.

    Discipline Healing Foundation Endurance Mental Health Chronic Pain

    I spent twelve years in the root stage without knowing that's what it was. Chronic pain. Identity loss. The long, invisible work of rebuilding something I couldn't see yet. It looked like nothing from the outside. It looked like dormancy or failure or being stuck.

    The root system is doing something the surface doesn't show. It's conserving. Deepening. Waiting for conditions that don't exist yet.

    The root stage is where character is built. Where faith is stripped of its performance layer and becomes something real. Where the disciplines of health — sleep, nutrition, movement, mental care — are established not for results but for survival. The roots don't look like progress. They are the condition for all progress that follows.

    In The Garden, we honor the root stage. We don't rush people through it. We sit with them in it.

    Stage Three

    The Bloom

    The visible evidence of becoming. Purpose finding form. Joy with roots. The fruit of patient, invisible work finally appearing above the soil.

    Purpose Family Fitness Financial Freedom Service Beauty

    The bloom is not the point of the plant. It is the evidence of the point. Everything underground — the root work, the patient tending, the seasons of nothing visible — was building toward this. Toward something that could open and be seen.

    The garden doesn't bloom to be admired. It blooms because it is healthy enough to. The bloom is the symptom of good roots.

    In a life, the bloom looks like this: a marriage that has weathered something and come out closer. A body that has been tended back to health. A faith that was tested and became more honest. A business that grew from genuine service. Financial freedom earned through patience and stewardship rather than luck or hustle.

    The bloom is beautiful. But we never forget what made it possible.

    Stage Four

    The Perennial

    Some seasons appear dormant. The blooms disappear. The color fades. But the roots continue growing. Every spring the plant returns stronger because of the winter it survived.

    Resilience Seasonality Community Knowledge Returning

    The perennial is my favorite thing in the garden. Not because it is the most dramatic. Because it is the most honest about how growth actually works. It doesn't pretend winter didn't happen. It comes back anyway — and often comes back fuller than it was before.

    Dormant is not dead. The root system is working. What looks like nothing is doing everything that matters.

    The perennial stage is for people who have been here before. Who have bloomed and gone dormant and come back. Who understand now that the seasons are not obstacles to growth — they are the mechanism of it. The people in this stage become the ones who help the seeds understand what is possible.

    Community is what the perennial stage produces. Not just personal resilience, but the wisdom to tend others through their winters.

    Stage Five

    The Annual

    Not everything returns the same way. Some seasons complete their purpose. But healthy annuals always leave seeds behind. Growth continues through legacy.

    Legacy Purpose Letting Go Passing On Faith

    The annual completes. It does not return the way the perennial returns. But before it goes it releases seeds — intentionally, abundantly — into the soil where the next season will begin. The annual's purpose was never only its own bloom. It was also the seeds it would leave behind.

    Sometimes environment — not potential — determines whether something thrives. The seed knows this and releases anyway, trusting the soil it lands in.

    This is the stage of legacy. Of recognizing that some of what we build is not for us — it is for the people who will come after us, who will find seeds we scattered in seasons we've already forgotten. A letter written to a child who can't read it yet. A garden inherited by someone you'll never meet. A conversation that changes a life you won't know about.

    The lifecycle of The Garden is not a straight line. It is a circle. The annual's seeds become next season's seed stage. The story begins again.
